The project in one minute

  1. What we built. OSCAR (IEEE ICC 2023) uses one deep-RL agent to tune the contention window of one Wi-Fi access point. We extended it to N access points in one shared ns-3 simulation, each AP with its own agent and its own contention window, scaled it from 2 to 10 APs, and built a full evaluation protocol around it fact.
  2. What we found first. One global contention window learned by OSCAR beats standard Wi-Fi backoff (BEB) by about +30 % at every size we tried. Independent per-AP agents do not: at 20,40 stations they are −5.6 ± 76 % against BEB, and they get worse as APs are added (−46 % at 10 APs) fact.
  3. What we found next. When all APs hear each other, a simple 2005 rule with no learning (IdleSense) ties OSCAR: 38.77 vs 38.65 Mbps, p = 0.31. In a 3-AP "flow-in-the-middle" chain, every simple method starves the middle AP, and per-AP learners with a fairness reward lift it from 1.44 to about 10 Mbps, close to the best fixed setting, at a cost in total throughput fact.
  4. So v4 aims at robustness. No single method is best everywhere. v4 trains one controller (RAFA) once, then tests it unchanged on a suite of scenarios, and scores it by how much of the gap between BEB and the best fixed setting it closes in each one. The claim is that it is never clearly worse than the best rival in any scenario and clearly better than each rival somewhere decision. This is a plan being tested, not a result yet. Its first milestone (v3.5.0, tooling) is done fact.

Part 1 · The basics from first principles

1.1 How Wi-Fi devices share one channel

A Wi-Fi network is an access point (AP) plus the stations (STAs) attached to it, such as laptops and phones. One AP and its stations form a BSS (basic service set). All of them talk on the same radio channel, and only one transmission can succeed at a time where two signals overlap. If two devices send at once, both frames are corrupted. That is a collision.

Wi-Fi has no central scheduler for normal traffic. Each device decides for itself when to send, using a rule called CSMA/CA (carrier-sense multiple access with collision avoidance):

  1. Listen first (carrier sense). If the channel is busy, wait.
  2. When the channel goes idle, wait a short fixed gap (AIFS, 43 µs for best-effort traffic in our setup).
  3. Then pick a random number of slots (9 µs each) between 0 and the contention window (CW), and count down only while the channel stays idle.
  4. Send when the counter reaches zero. If no acknowledgement (ACK) comes back, assume a collision and try again.

The central trade-off. A small CW means short waits but many devices pick the same number, so collisions rise. A large CW means few collisions but the channel sits idle while everyone counts down. The best CW grows with the number of devices competing. That single number is what this whole project tunes.

1.2 BEB, the standard rule, and why it is not optimal

Binary exponential backoff (BEB) is what 802.11 does by default. Every device starts at a small CWmin (15 or 16 in our code), and after each failed attempt it doubles its CW up to CWmax (1023). After a success it resets to CWmin. BEB needs no knowledge of how many devices exist, which is why it is the standard.

Its weakness: every device returns to the small CWmin after each success, so in a crowded network the first attempt of almost every frame collides again. With 60 saturated stations, BEB in our simulator gets about 27.3 Mbps total, while the best fixed CW (512 for all) gets 39.6 Mbps fact. That 40 % gap is the headroom every method in this project tries to capture.

1.3 Reinforcement learning and DDPG in plain words

Reinforcement learning (RL) learns a behaviour by trial and error. An agent sees an observation (here, a number describing how congested its AP is), takes an action (picks a CW), and receives a reward (here, how much data got through). Over many steps it learns a policy: a mapping from observation to action that earns high reward.

DDPG (deep deterministic policy gradient) is an RL algorithm for continuous actions. It has two small neural networks that together make one agent:

  • The actor takes the observation and outputs the action.
  • The critic estimates how good an (observation, action) pair is, i.e. the expected future reward. The actor is trained to choose actions the critic rates highly.
  • Past experience is stored in a replay buffer and sampled in batches. Slow-moving target networks keep training stable. During training a little random exploration noise is added to actions.

Our DDPG is the one OSCAR uses, unchanged: actor and critic each 128 × 128 hidden units, discount γ = 0.7, soft update τ = 0.001, batch 32, noise σ = 0.1, the first 300 steps random warm-up fact. Important for your mentor: "actor + critic" is one agent, not two agents.

TD3 is an improved DDPG (two critics, delayed actor updates). We tested it as an option in v3.4.0.

1.4 Multi-agent RL and the words that go with it

  • Independent learners: each AP has its own agent, own replay buffer, own reward, and sees only its own data. This is our default "multi-agent" mode. It is decentralised, but not cooperative.
  • Parameter sharing ("shared" mode): one network is used by every AP. Each AP still feeds it its own observation and gets its own CW, but all APs train the same weights.
  • Team reward: every agent gets the same reward computed from all APs. This makes agents cooperate, but each AP then indirectly uses information about the others during training.
  • CTDE / central critic (centralised training, decentralised execution, the idea behind MADDPG): during training one critic sees all APs; at run time each actor still acts on local data only.

Why multi-agent is hard here: the APs share one channel, so each AP's best CW depends on what the others do. With selfish rewards, each agent is tempted to lower its CW to grab airtime, which raises collisions for everyone. That is a game, not a single optimisation problem inference, and it is the main reason independent agents did badly (Part 3).

1.5 OSCAR, the paper we extend

OSCAR (Grasso, Raftopoulos, Schembra, IEEE ICC 2023) runs one DDPG agent at a single AP. Every 10 ms of simulated time it reads the recent packet-loss ratio and outputs one CW, which is applied to the AP and all its stations with CWmin = CWmax (so the BEB doubling is turned off). The reward is normalised goodput lit. The paper reports +11 % (15 stations) to +40 % (50 stations) throughput over 802.11, +15 to +30 % over CCOD (an earlier DRL method), convergence in about 300 steps, and gives only relative numbers, no absolute Mbps, no fairness, delay or collision figures lit. So we never compare our Mbps to the paper's; we compare paired gains over BEB inside our own simulator decision.

Action mapping kept from OSCAR: the actor outputs a ∈ [−1, 1], scaled to 0..6, and CW = 2^(4 + a), so the CW range is 16 to 1024 fact.

1.6 IdleSense, the strong heuristic rival

IdleSense (Heusse et al., SIGCOMM 2005) is a non-learning rule. Theory says throughput is near-maximal when the average number of idle slots between transmission attempts equals a fixed target that depends only on the PHY timing. So each device counts idle slots and steers toward the target lit:

  • After every 5 of its own transmissions, compute the mean idle slots n̂.
  • If n̂ is below the target (channel too busy): CW ← 1.2 × CW.
  • Otherwise: CW ← 2·CW / (2 + 0.001·CW), a gentle decrease.

For our PHY the paper's formula gives a target of about 3.3 (we estimated the collision time, so this is an approximation) fact T_c estimated. It is decentralised, needs no training, and assumes every device hears every other one.

1.7 Fairness, PF utility and the flow-in-the-middle problem

Total throughput can hide a starved AP. We therefore use three numbers per run:

  • Aggregate: total Mbps of all APs.
  • min-AP: the Mbps of the worst-off AP.
  • PF utility: the sum over APs of ln(Mbps). This is "proportional fairness": doubling a small AP's rate is worth as much as doubling a large one's, so starving anyone costs a lot. Example: 23.18, 13.25, 23.15 Mbps gives ln 23.18 + ln 13.25 + ln 23.15 = 8.87.

Jain's index (1 = perfectly equal) is reported too. α-fairness generalises this: α = 0 is plain total throughput, α = 1 is PF, α = 2 and above lean further toward the worst-off.

Our hard scenario is 10,10,10 --apHidden 1-3. The two edge BSSs cannot hear each other, so they transmit independently and their airtime overlaps freely. The middle BSS hears both, so it must defer whenever either edge is sending, and the channel it sees is almost never idle. Under BEB the middle AP gets 1.44 Mbps while each edge gets about 35 fact. The literature calls this flow-in-the-middle (Gawłowicz et al.) lit. It is related to, but not the same as, the classic hidden-terminal problem. The fix is asymmetric: the edges must back off (high CW) and the middle should be aggressive (low CW). One common CW cannot do that, which is why it is the scenario where per-AP control can matter.

1.8 The simulator: ns-3 and ns3-gym

ns-3 is a packet-level network simulator used widely in research. It simulates every frame, backoff slot, collision and ACK with the real 802.11 MAC logic. We use ns-3.29 (the version OSCAR used) with 802.11ax at 5 GHz, one 20 MHz channel, fixed rate HE-MCS 11, uplink UDP traffic of 1464-byte packets from each station to its AP fact. Default load is 150 Mbps per station, far above capacity, so every station always has data (saturation).

ns3-gym connects ns-3 to Python using the OpenAI Gym interface over a ZeroMQ socket. Every 10 ms of simulated time, ns-3 pauses, sends the observation and reward to Python, waits for the action, and continues. A standard run is 15 s of simulated time = 1500 steps. All numbers are measured in the policy window, steps 300 and later (after 3 s) fact.

Part 2 · What we built: the system

2.1 Architecture

One ns-3 process holds every AP and station, so their transmissions really interfere fact. Python holds the agents. The number of APs and stations per AP is a run-time flag (--apStaCounts 20,40). All APs share one collision domain by default: the loss model gives 50 dB between every pair of nodes, so positions do not matter and everyone hears everyone fact. The hidden chain is an opt-in exception (ADR-0005).

2.4 Scenarios and the evaluation protocol

  • Topologies run: 2,3 · 5,15 · 20,20 · 20,40 (main one-domain case) · 4 APs 5,10,15,20 · 6 APs 5..30 · 8 APs 5..40 · 10 APs 4,8,...,40 (220 STAs) · fixed 60 STAs over 2/4/6/10 APs · imbalance 5,55 and 5,5,5,45 · 6×10 · 22×10 · loads 0.5, 1, 150 Mbps per STA · the hidden chain 10,10,10 with equal and uneven loads fact.
  • Early protocol (v1.x to v3.0): train and measure in the same run (the agent is still exploring), 3 to 5 seeds, mean ± 95 % CI, paired by seed against BEB.
  • Fair protocol (v3.2.0 onward): train for 2 episodes, save the actor, then evaluate it frozen (no noise, no learning) on 8 held-out evaluation seeds (python 100 to 107, ns-3 101 to 108). Every method runs on the same seeds, so differences are paired. Test: exact two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank; with n = 8 the smallest possible p is 0.0078, reached when all 8 differences have the same sign fact.
  • Policy seeds: from v3.4.0 we retrain with different training seeds (0, 1, 2), because two trainings of the same method can give different policies. This turned out to matter a lot (Part 4).
  • Validation habits: after every simulator change we re-check fixed regression numbers (single-AP dry run = 10533 packets, 5,15 = 9000, OSCAR smoke reward 10.59458) and byte-identical traces for default flags. Every training run uses --check, which asserts per-AP isolation, CW readback and reward formulas every step fact.

3.3 Central critic (v1.7.0 to v1.9.0, Oct 16 to 17)

Hypothesis: a central critic that sees all APs during training (CTDE, ADR-0003) would stop the selfish game. Built as --mode central and evaluated at 4 and 10 APs, 3 seeds. Result: negative. At 10 APs central was −5.80 ± 10.48 Mbps versus independent and collapsed to about 3.2 Mbps, below BEB fact. Follow-ups (v2.5.2 to v2.7.0) showed the collapse is linked to the tiny reward scale: at 22×10, reward ×100 avoided it (38.0 Mbps, Jain 0.98, n = 1) while ×3.67 did not fact. It was recorded as an optional negative result, as the design allowed.

3.5 Trying to beat OSCAR (v3.0.0 to v3.2.0, Oct 25 to 28)

After a research note on why independent agents lose (selfish CW game, no BEB doubling, a noisy one-number observation, rewards near zero at saturation, measuring while still exploring) inference, three steps followed:

  • v3.0.0: history observation, reward mixing (--alpha), reward ×100 and parameter sharing (ADR-0004). Only sharing reached Global level. Best variant V5 at 20,40: 37.08 ± 0.85 vs Global 36.17 ± 6.95, +2.8 ± 16.9 % (CI spans 0). Its per-AP CWs did not separate, so it acts like one global CW with lower seed variance fact.
  • v3.1.0 ceiling: 50 static runs. At 20,40 the best fixed common CW (512) gives 39.62 ± 0.21 Mbps, +12.3 over BEB but only +3.45 ± 6.77 over Global. No per-AP split beat the common CW. Conclusion: in one collision domain there is no headroom for per-AP CWs fact. This is what pushed the project toward the hidden chain.
  • v3.2.0 fair protocol: multi-episode training, save/load actor, frozen evaluation, TD3 and --cwMinOnly options, a runner for paired batches. Pilot at 20,40: frozen shared policy 39.74 vs BEB 27.33 (n = 1).

How to say Part 4 in two sentences. Where every AP hears every other, the problem is already handled by a simple rule, so learning adds nothing measurable. Where the best CW differs per AP, simple rules fail on fairness and a per-AP learner with a fairness reward gets close to the best fixed setting, but how close depends on the training seed.

Part 5 · Is this already solved? Novelty and industry evidence

5.1 Recent research (2023 to 2026)

A check of 15 papers from 2023 to 2026, on top of a 70-entry survey (29 Oct), found the topic is more active since OSCAR, not closed lit:

Both must be read before the final report.

5.2 Industry and standards

  • The problem is live: Qualcomm's 2025 Wi-Fi 8 paper names "overlapping signals and airtime contention" in dense networks; Cisco Meraki and Juniper Mist docs describe congestion and devices pausing transmission source.
  • Wi-Fi 8 (802.11bn) is still a draft, final approval expected about 2028; its targets include 25 % lower p95 latency in overlapping networks. Its CW-related changes are narrow and rule-based (high-priority EDCA) source.
  • Products tune radio settings, not backoff: Meraki AutoRF, Aruba AirMatch and Mist RRM adjust channel, power, width and band; Mist uses RL, but for those settings. CW/EDCA parameters are configurable mostly as static profiles; Huawei documents an optional "dynamic EDCA" for high density without saying how it works source.
  • Exact wording to use: "We did not find public product documentation explicitly advertising learned contention-window optimization." Do not say "no product does it". Keep RF interference, co-channel interference, airtime contention and MAC backoff separate: CW tuning only addresses the last two in part.
  • Gaps in that research: no independent statistics on how common the problem is; Broadcom primary release, WBA article and Qualcomm OnQ blog not read; figures not visually checked.
Part 6 · v4, explained separately

v4 in five sentences

  1. Every method we have fails somewhere: IdleSense and Global-CW starve the middle AP in the chain, static settings need a per-scenario search, and per-scenario learners must be retrained for each case.
  2. v4 builds one controller, RAFA, trains it once over a pool of randomised scenarios, and never retrains it.
  3. It is scored per scenario by gap closed: 0 means BEB, 1 means the best fixed setting for that scenario.
  4. Every method must respect a fair floor so that no AP is starved to inflate the total.
  5. The claim: RAFA is never more than 0.05 GC below the best rival in any scenario, and significantly better than each rival in at least one.

Status: plan revision 6 (1 Nov). Milestone M0 is done and tagged v3.5.0 on feature/v4; dev has not received the v4 work yet. Next is M1 fact.

6.1 The metric: gap closed (GC)

For one scenario and one fairness level α:

GC = (U − U_BEB) / (U_oracle − U_BEB), U_α = Σ_i n_i · u_α( max(ε, x_i / n_i) )

  • x_i is AP i's throughput, n_i its station count, so x_i/n_i is throughput per station. ε = 0.01 Mbps per station avoids log(0) for a starved AP.
  • u_α is the α-fair utility: α = 0 is plain throughput, α = 1 is log (PF), α = 2 is −1/x. Weighting by n_i fixes a flaw found in the old PF reward, which was unweighted and would give a 20-station BSS and a 40-station BSS equal shares fact.
  • The oracle is the best static per-AP CW for that scenario that still satisfies the fair floor, found by a grid search on selection seeds. It is a reference, not a rival.
  • Edge cases fixed in advance: if U_oracle − U_BEB is under 3 BEB seed SDs (for example at low load, where everyone delivers everything), the cell is scored on the raw metric with a 1 % margin. Delay cells use GC on log p99 delay.

Why GC? A +40 % gain over BEB sounds big, but at 20,40 IdleSense gets it with no learning. GC asks the fairer question: of the improvement that was available in this scenario, how much did you capture?

6.2 The fair floor

floor_i = min(BEB_i, BEB aggregate · n_i / Σn). In words: no AP may get less than it would under standard BEB, or less than its station-weighted share of BEB's total, whichever is smaller. A result is infeasible in a cell if any AP gets below 0.95 · floor_i. It is still reported, but cannot win the cell. Unconstrained results are shown beside the constrained ones, so a reader can apply their own rule.

Revision 6 added the 5 % slack because with the strict floor the chain's own static oracle (256,128,256) was itself infeasible: its edge APs got 23.2 Mbps against a floor of 23.88, 2.9 % short fact. IdleSense at 0.53 Mbps for the middle AP (floor about 1.44) is infeasible in the chain inference from the definition.

6.6 Statistics

  • Three disjoint seed sets: selection (python 0–2) for tuning and oracle choice; evaluation (python 100–109); training (ns-3 2001 and up).
  • Two stages: every method on 3 eval seeds, then the top 3 feasible baselines per cell plus each baseline's weakest cell, and both RAFA ensembles, to 10 seeds.
  • Claim 1: intersection-union non-inferiority test, margin 0.05 GC, every cell at 0.05. Claim 2: paired t with Holm correction over all (cell, baseline) pairs. Wilcoxon reported beside.
  • An analysis spec (metric code, margins, cells, seeds, tests) is committed before M4 as pre-registration.

Part 7 · Limitations and claims to avoid

7.1 Limitations to state yourself

  • Simulator: ns-3.29's early 802.11ax model (no OFDMA or MU-EDCA), one 20 MHz channel, fixed rate, uplink constant-rate UDP only, no mobility.
  • Topology model: by default everyone hears everyone (positions do not matter). The chain is a coarse all-or-nothing BSS-level loss matrix, not a building model. No partial overlap, no distance-based path loss.
  • Observation: the default loss ratio counts ACKs and beacons, so it is a proxy. A legacy logger names it "Current Collision Rate"; it is not a collision rate.
  • Metrics on dev: no collision, retransmission, delay or queue metrics. Delay and Retry traces exist only from v3.5.0 on feature/v4.
  • Statistics: early versions used 3 to 5 seeds measured during learning, with wide CIs. The chain results use 8 eval seeds but at most 3 training seeds per arm; no hypothesis test across training seeds. Training was only 2 episodes.
  • Inherited quirks left unchanged: the C++ per-flow Jain index uses uninitialised variables (our reported Jain is computed in Python and is fine); "sentMbytes" is Mbit.
  • Repo hygiene: tags v0.1.0 and v0.2.0 are missing on GitHub; v2.8.2 is not reachable from main or dev; v4 work sits on feature/v4, not yet on dev.

7.2 Claims to avoid (from the Review 2 audit)

  • "Independent agents beat BEB or OSCAR." They do not (−5.6 ± 76 % at 20,40, worse with more APs).
  • "Cooperative multi-agent RL" or "MADDPG" for the default path. The default is independent DDPG; cooperative variants are opt-in; the MADDPG-style central critic was a negative result.
  • "Reduces collisions, retransmissions or latency." Not measured on dev.
  • "Models co-channel interference, spatial reuse or realistic dense deployments."
  • "Robust" or "generalises to unseen scenarios." That is the v4 hypothesis, not yet a result.
  • "Matches the static optimum." Not stable across training seeds.
  • Absolute Mbps comparison with the OSCAR paper, which reports only relative gains.
Part 8 · Likely mentor questions with honest answers
What is your contribution, in one sentence?

"We extended OSCAR to a multi-AP ns-3 testbed with per-AP agents and strong baselines, showed where per-AP learning does and does not help, and in v4 we test whether one trained controller can be near-best in every scenario of a suite without retraining."

So far the evidence-backed contributions are: the multi-AP environment and protocol, the negative result for independent agents, the finding that IdleSense ties OSCAR in one domain, and the fairness result in the chain. The robustness claim is the v4 hypothesis.

OSCAR is from 2023. Isn't this already solved?

No. At least ten 2024 to 2026 papers work on learned contention control, Wi-Fi 8 is still a draft until about 2028, and the commercial RRM we checked tunes channel and power, not CW. Most papers compare only with BEB in one setup with a model trained for that setup. Cite drlCW, Hao et al. and Nunez et al. as closest work before you are asked (Part 5).

Did your multi-agent system beat OSCAR?

In one collision domain, no. Independent agents are below BEB on average and much worse at 10 APs; shared agents reach OSCAR's level but not beyond (+2.8 ± 16.9 %, CI spans 0); the best fixed CW is only about 1 Mbps above OSCAR, so there is little to gain. In the chain, per-AP learners with a PF reward beat OSCAR (Global-CW) on fairness (+0.68 PF, +7.4 Mbps for the weakest AP) at a cost of 13.6 Mbps total.

Why do independent agents do worse than BEB?

Most likely reasons inference, partly tested: each agent maximises its own goodput, so lowering its CW grabs airtime and hurts others (a selfish game); the action sets CWmin = CWmax, removing BEB's doubling safety net; the observation is one noisy scalar; rewards are near zero at saturation; and results were measured while agents still explored. Fixes tested in v3.0 (history, reward mixing, ×100, sharing) only helped once parameters were shared.

Why does a 2005 heuristic tie your RL?

In one collision domain the optimal CW depends mainly on how many stations compete, and IdleSense measures exactly the right signal (idle slots) to find it. RL has nothing extra to learn there. That is why v4 judges methods against IdleSense and the best fixed CW, not BEB.

Isn't the PF win in the chain built in, since you reward PF?

Partly, yes, and we say so: the reward and the metric are aligned. That is why we compare against the static oracle (the best any fixed setting can do on PF), report the aggregate cost, and in v4 score every method at several fairness levels (α = 0, 1, 2) with the same fair floor.

Is the hidden chain realistic?

It is a coarse model: the two edge BSSs are completely deaf to each other (200 dB), every other pair hears perfectly (50 dB). It captures the flow-in-the-middle effect known in the literature, not any real building. ADR-0005 records this as a deliberate, user-approved exception.

Why DDPG, and not DQN, PPO or MAPPO?

DDPG is what OSCAR uses, so keeping it isolates the multi-AP extension. TD3 was tested and its ranking against DDPG flipped between seeds. MAPPO needs far more episodes than 10-minute ns-3 jobs allow, and the CTDE central critic collapsed at 10 APs.

How do you know the results are not noise?

Every method runs on the same 8 held-out seeds, so we test paired differences; with all 8 differences of one sign the exact Wilcoxon p is 0.008. We also retrained with 3 training seeds and found that seed-to-seed spread is larger than the eval CIs, which is why we only keep claims that hold for every seed. v4 adds 10 eval seeds, two ensembles, multiple-comparison correction and a pre-registered analysis.

Why did the central critic fail?

At 10 APs it collapsed to about 3 Mbps. Follow-ups point to the tiny reward scale (×100 avoided the collapse at 22×10, n = 1). The exact cause was not fully tested. It was designed as optional and is reported as a negative result.

Could a real AP do this?

An AP can advertise EDCA parameters to its stations in beacons, but only CW values of 2^k − 1 and only once per beacon interval (102.4 ms), while our agents act every 10 ms. v4 measures the rounding effect and has an optional beacon-interval deployability check (M6). Compatibility with 802.11bn mechanisms was not studied.

Do you measure collisions or delay?

Not on dev. v3.5.0 (on feature/v4) added Retry-bit rate, failed attempts and per-AP delay mean/p50/p99; v4 scenario S7 scores p99 delay.

What if v4 does not work?

Each milestone ends in a reportable result, and the plan has kill rules: fall back to independent actors or to per-scenario robustness. A well-tested negative or narrower result is still a valid finding, and the suite table with GC for all existing methods (M1) is useful on its own.

Is the compute feasible?

The minimum complete result (M0 to M4) is estimated at about 210 serial hours, roughly 70 h wall-clock with 3 lanes. M0 already showed jobs run 2.5 to 3 times faster at the lower load. The deadline is not recorded in the repo, so the plan fixes a drop order unknown deadline.

Why is it relevant to industry?

Vendors and the Wi-Fi 8 work describe airtime contention in overlapping networks as a live problem; products tune channel and power, and CW/EDCA is mostly static. Whether learned CW control helps in practice is our hypothesis, and our own mixed results must be shown next to that argument.
